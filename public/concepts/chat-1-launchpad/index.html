AI chat page · wireframe 1 of 3

Launchpad

The suggestion, drawn: the page opens on four example prompts and twelve theme chips above a centred chat box. Everything on this cold canvas exists to answer the one question an empty box never answers - what would I even ask? - and the chips go one step further than suggesting, by building the first message out of what the user picks.

Notation. Dashed outline = empty or not built yet. Solid hairline = real chrome. Grey bar = body copy nobody is judging. Blue = the one live action on the canvas. Amber number = a note in the panel beside the frame, which is commentary and not part of the UI. No art and no brand colour anywhere: this is a wireframe, not a mockup.

Nothing sent yet. This is the state the chat page fails at today (a text box and a placeholder), and the state the suggestion is about, so it gets the whole screen: title, prompts, themes, box - in that order, which is the order the suggestion asked for.

Notes · not part of the UI

1 Lead with the question, not the caret

An empty box asks "what do you know?" and most people do not know. The heading asks something answerable, and the prompts under it are the answer to "answerable how?".

2 One live action on the whole canvas

Send is the only blue thing on the screen. Everything else is a suggestion, so nothing competes with the box for the next click.

3 A row fills the box, it does not send

Clicking an example puts it in the composer and leaves the caret there, so the user can edit it - half the value of an example is that it can be changed. The row is a sentence in the product's voice, and the product cannot know whether they meant four players or eight.

4 Chips are vocabulary, not options

Twelve chips is about the most a newcomer will read. They are the words of the domain - game types and moods - labelled so it is obvious they are a starting point and not a menu with a wrong answer in it.

State 1b — three chips picked

The part of the suggestion that does the real work. Picking chips does not just hint at a game, it assembles the opening message in a tray above the box, in the user's own order of thought. It is the difference between a page that suggests and a page that starts.

Notes · not part of the UI

5 The tray turns picking into a sentence

Three chips and the brief line under them are the seed of the first message, and Write it for me commits it. The user did the thinking; the page did the phrasing, which is the one thing a beginner cannot do yet.

Two decisions to make here: the tray replaces the example rows once it has anything in it (or the page carries both), and the phrase is editable after it is written - a brief the user cannot correct is worse than no brief.

State 2 — after the first message

The shelf cannot survive the first send - the transcript needs the room - so it collapses to one row of starters, and the themes come back where they are worth most: as reply chips on the assistant's follow-up question. The plan the chat produces arrives in the transcript, in the same column the user has been reading.

Notes · not part of the UI

6 The starters survive the first send

The shelf becomes a rail, and the themes come back as reply chips inside the question - where they are genuinely useful, because "do you want them to fight back?" is a question most people cannot answer in prose either. Same pattern, better moment.

7 The plan lives in the transcript

No side panel and no second place to look: what is produced is what was being read. The price is that the plan scrolls away with the conversation, so a chat of any length needs a way back to it - Your plan so far is also a link, and it should not be a long scroll to find it.

On a phone

This is where the suggestion gets hard. Four prompt rows and twelve chips is roughly a screen and a half of suggestions, and the box they are meant to lead to sits below all of it. So on a phone the shelf scrolls in the region above the box, the box is docked to the foot of the screen, and the two "all" links stay where they can be reached.

Notes · not part of the UI

8 The composer is docked, not parked

The suggestion puts the box last, and on a phone that means below a screen of suggestions. So the box is the last row of the screen and the shelf scrolls in the region above it - the order the owner asked for, with the box reachable at every moment.

9 The shelf shows its head, and keeps its doors

Three examples and eight chips, cut with a fade, because the full shelf is roughly a screen and a half and the box has to stay reachable. The two "all" links move out of the lists on a phone and sit permanently just above the box: at 400px a link at the foot of a scrolling list is a link nobody sees, and those two links are the only way to the rest of the shelf.

This is the part of the suggestion most worth testing on a real phone. The shortened shelf is quick to read, and it hides the two prompt rows most likely to teach a beginner what this chat is for.

What this direction bets on

Every direction in this set is a bet about which failure the chat page has to solve first. This one bets that the failure is the empty box, and pays for that bet with screen space above it.

Good at

  • Teaching a beginner what to ask, which no plain chat box does.
  • Giving away the product's own vocabulary - the chips are the answer to "what kinds of games are there?".
  • Showing the shape of the output before anything is typed.
  • Costing nothing to learn: it is still a chat box, with a menu above it.

Risks

  • The box trails two blocks of suggestions, so on a short window it is the last thing on screen - the shelf has to fit, or the composer has to pin.
  • Example prompts date fast, and stale examples make a product look abandoned.
  • A shelf of four can read as "these are the only things you can ask".
  • Chips can turn a conversation into a form, which is the opposite of the point.

What to instrument

  • Prompt rows clicked vs. typed straight into the box.
  • How many chips get picked before the first send - and whether the tray's brief line is edited before it is sent.
  • Whether a seeded first message leads to a finished plan more often than a typed one.
  • How often the starters rail is used after the first exchange: if never, it is decoration.
