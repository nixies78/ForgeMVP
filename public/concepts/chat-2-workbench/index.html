Workbench

The chat is the process, the design is the product: a live design panel fills in beside the conversation. The box stays where it is, in the left column, and everything the conversation produces - the game type, the UEFN devices, the five build steps - lands in a panel on the right while the user is still talking.

Notation. Dashed outline = empty or not built yet. Solid hairline = real chrome. Grey bar = body copy nobody is judging. Blue = the one live action on the canvas. Amber number = a note in the panel beside the frame, which is commentary and not part of the UI. No art and no brand colour anywhere: this is a wireframe, not a mockup.

Notes · not part of the UI

1 Three lines instead of a hero

The cold canvas here is quieter than Launchpad's on purpose. The panel is doing the teaching - it names the five questions Forge will ask - so the chat side only has to say what to type, that questions follow, and what comes out. The third line is the promise, and it is stated before anything is typed: the game type, the devices, five build steps.

2 Three rows, not twelve

Examples earn their space by covering the three ways someone arrives: a place and a mode, a mode on its own, and a rewrite of something they already have. Anything longer belongs in the panel's column, which is the trade this direction makes. The cost is real - a beginner gets less vocabulary here than on the Launchpad - and the quick picks are what pay it back.

3 Quick picks sit on the composer

They are the last thing above the box because they are a way into the box, and they are read in the order a brief is actually built: the mode, then the players, then how long a round runs, then the mood. Tapping one writes the phrase into the composer and leaves the caret there, so a pick is an edit and never a blind send.

4 The panel names what it will ask for

Five slots, all empty, all dashed, from the first second. Naming them turns "what is this thing going to ask me?" into a list the user can read before committing to anything, and the counts - 0 of 6, 0 of 5 - are a promise that they will move. The panel is drawn in real chrome with dashed contents: the frame is built, the answers are not.

5 One hint, one input

Exactly one instruction on the empty panel, at its foot, after the five empty rows. Clicking a slot routes back into the chat rather than opening a form, so there is one input mechanism with two doors into it. That is deliberate: the moment a slot can be filled in directly, the panel becomes a second form and the conversation stops being the process.

State 2 — after the first exchange

One idea, one follow-up answered, and a second turn. The transcript is short and the panel is not: that imbalance is the direction working as designed. The chat never repeats the plan back - the plan is over there, with counts on it and an action under it.

Notes · not part of the UI

6 A tap answers; the message is the record

The three taps sit inside the question that produced them, so answering costs one click and no typing. What the tap writes is a real user turn in the transcript - "Clock runs out. Ten minutes is the whole round." - which the user can edit like any other sentence, and which the panel then mirrors. The panel never becomes the place where the truth lives: it is a read-out of the conversation, and if the two ever disagree the transcript wins.

7 Draft is a state, not a warning

Prop Hunt - Draft matches the app's own vocabulary: game types are live or draft, and a draft resolves to the category's baseline devices until it is researched. Saying so on the slot is the honest version of a plan that is 13 researched types and 74 drafts deep - better than presenting a draft as if it were finished.

8 Five devices, two of them only weighed

The list fills in as the conversation decides things, and it distinguishes a device the plan has chosen from one the AI is only weighing. Considered is a promise that the user can strike it out; printing all five as decided would be the AI claiming a decision it has not been given. The sixth stays empty on purpose - the End Game Device has no built-in win condition, it is wired from whatever ends the round, so it cannot be added until the caught question is answered.

9 Open questions point back into the chat

Two things the AI still needs, named rather than guessed at, each one a route back into the conversation. The panel is a to-do list for the chat and not a second form: the user answers in the composer - or clicks one of these to be asked about it - and the list shortens itself when the answer lands.

10 The action is disabled, and says why

Build in UEFN is the reason the panel exists, so it lives at the panel's foot rather than in the transcript. It is greyed while the plan is unfinished and the reason is next to it, in words: two open questions. A disabled button with no reason is a dead end, and this one is two clicks from being enabled, which is why the accent on this canvas is on the composer's send instead - the thing that can actually be done.

The cost of this split — no marker

Two panes are two places to look. Anyone who only wants a chat has a column of furniture beside them for the whole conversation, and a short exchange leaves the left column visibly emptier than the right. If the plan is finished in ten words, the panel was 280px spent on a summary nobody needed.

A panel that is wrong is also worse than prose that is wrong: printing Prop Hunt - Draft with a device count commits the product to a game type the user may have been half-joking about. That is the argument for the counts being small, the state being named, and every device being strikeable.

On a phone

At 400px the split is impossible - two columns of 190px makes both of them useless - so the panel stops being a column and becomes a peer of the chat: a tab, and a sheet that rises from the foot of it. The tab carries the count, so the filling in is visible without opening anything. The composer is the last row of the canvas in both tabs, and that is the rule this state exists to check.

Notes · not part of the UI

11 At 400px the panel is a tab, not a column

Two panes side by side at 400px is two 190px columns, and a chat bubble at 190px is not readable. So the panel becomes a peer of the chat - one tap away, never beside it - and the tab carries the count it is hiding: Design (5). Someone who never opens the tab can still see the design filling in, which is the part of the direction worth keeping on a phone.

12 The sheet is a summary, and says so

What survives the move to a phone: the game type and its state, the player count, the device count with the list and its considered rows, the build-step count, the open questions and the disabled action. What does not: the five build steps' wording, collapsed to a count, and the Brand slot, which is optional and is the one slot a phone user is least likely to have an answer for. A sheet that scrolls to three screens is not a sheet, and the steps are the one part of the panel that reads better as a list in the transcript later.

13 The composer does not move

The whole point of the tab is that the panel is the thing that changes. If opening it pushed the box off the screen or made the user close a sheet to keep talking, this direction would fail at exactly the width where it is most often used. The box is the last row of the canvas in both tabs, and the questions the sheet is holding are answerable from it.

What this direction bets on

This one bets that the feeling of progress matters as much as the words, and pays for it with half the screen. If the plan is only ever read once, at the end, the panel is 280px of furniture and the transcript was right all along.

Good at

  • Making the output feel like the product rather than the residue of a conversation - the plan is on screen while it is still being argued about.
  • Answering "what will it ask me?" before it asks: the five slots are the whole interview, named in advance.
  • Showing change without making the user re-read: the device count, the ticked steps and the shortening question list are all one glance.
  • Progress that is a state rather than a scroll position - useful in a chat that can run long.
  • Surviving a long session: the transcript can be scrolled past, and the plan cannot be lost in it.

Risks

  • Two panes are two places to look. The panel is dead weight for anyone who only wants a chat, and there is no version of this direction where it is not.
  • Choosing a game type out loud is a bigger commitment than saying it in a sentence, and the panel is where that commitment becomes visible.
  • The panel can outgrow the conversation, leaving the chat column as blank paper exactly when the user has stopped reading the plan.
  • On a phone the design hides behind a tab, and a tab label that goes stale - Design (5) that never becomes (6) - reads as broken.
  • The device list invites a second form: once "considered" rows can be swapped, someone will want a picker, and a picker for 59 devices is a worse experience than a sentence.

What to instrument if it ships

  • How often the panel is opened or scrolled, and from which turn. The right column is furniture on the day nobody touches it.
  • Considered devices kept, struck out or ignored - the only honest measure of whether the AI's device picks are trusted.
  • Slot clicks against typed answers. If nobody ever clicks a slot, the hint is decoration and the panel is a read-only display.
  • Open questions answered against open questions abandoned: the panel's list is the shortest measurable path to a finished plan.
  • Plans that reach Build in UEFN enabled, and how many of them are draft game types rather than the 13 researched ones.
