Interview

The AI speaks first and every answer is a tap. The blank box is the failure mode - it asks a user to bring the question, the vocabulary and the confidence before Forge can help with any of them - so there is no blank box: the page opens as the first turn of a conversation that has already started, one answerable question with six tappable answers, and typing offered as a link at the edge of it.

Notation. Dashed outline = empty, placeholder or not built yet. Grey bar = body copy nobody is judging. Blue = the one live action on the canvas. Amber numbered marker = a note in the panel to the right of the frame, and the notes are commentary rather than part of the UI. Two colours of meaning, no art and no brand colour anywhere: this is a wireframe, not a mockup.

State 1 — the first turn

The screen a user meets on a cold canvas, which is the state the chat page fails at today. There is no title, no empty box and no placeholder to get past: the assistant's first line stands where the page's heading would be, and the only thing left to do is answer one question with one tap. Everything else in the canvas is either a way out of that question or the receipt for the answers.

Notes · not part of the UI

1 A page that has already started

The failure this direction is built around is not that people cannot type. It is that they cannot type first: an empty box asks the user to bring the question, the vocabulary and the confidence at the same time, and most people arrive with one of the three.

So the assistant's first line stands where the page's title would be, and nothing on the canvas is waiting for the user to begin. The trade is made honestly: Forge claims the opening move, and the user answers rather than authors.

2 One question, six answers, one tap each

“What should a player be doing in the first 30 seconds?” is answerable by anyone who has played a game, and it is the question underneath most of the 13 researched game types in the library: run and hide, fight, build, explore, race, solve.

Six covers the shapes. A seventh word is the one nobody reads, and a text field here would put the hardest part of design - naming the core loop - in front of a user who came to be told what the options are.

3 Not knowing is a valid answer

“Not sure - surprise me” is a real reply rather than a dead end. It keeps the interview moving, it hands the choice to Forge, and it is the cheapest tour of the library a beginner can take. A wizard with a required field could not accept this answer; a conversation can, and that is the whole difference.

4 Typing is a side door, not the doorway

The link is smaller and quieter than the cards on purpose. Someone who already knows what they want - a boss fight on a moving train - should not have to answer a quiz to say it, but the majority should not be invited to start on a blank page either.

This is the honest cost of leading with taps: the free-text path is real, and it is easy to walk past. Note 11 names what that costs.

5 The composer is demoted, not deleted

One line at the foot of the canvas, dashed because free text goes there, and the send control is the only blue thing on this screen. No answer card is highlighted: the assistant has no business recommending an answer to a question about the user's own game, so the six cards stay equal and the colour is spent on the one control that commits.

6 Five dots are a receipt

Goal, Feel, Players, Devices, Steps is what Forge intends to know before it writes a plan, in the order it will ask. The strip in the canvas foot is the user's view of that list, and it is not a progress bar: 0 of 5 is not a score, it is a statement of what has not been said yet.

It is also the reason the interview can be trusted to end. A user who can see five questions will answer five questions; a quiz with no visible end is the thing people abandon.

State 2 — three answers in

Three taps later. Every answer is a card in the transcript with an Edit beside it, Forge reads the brief back in its own words before it builds anything, and the plan arrives in the same column the user has been reading - the same game type, the same kind of device list and the same five build steps as the other two directions, so the difference being judged is where it lives, not what it is.

Notes · not part of the UI

7 This is a transcript, not a wizard

This is the line between Interview and the question wizard already in the product (questions-1 to questions-9). A wizard replaces the screen and forgets: answer 1 is gone when question 2 appears, and a wrong tap can only be undone by going back. Here every turn stays on screen with an Edit beside it, the user can argue in prose at any point, and Forge is allowed to deviate from the five slots when the game needs it.

The cost is honesty about state. A wizard always knows which step it is on; a conversation has to keep its own record, and that record is exactly two things - the editable cards, and the strip at the foot. The Edit beside a question is the same affordance pointed the other way: it means “ask me something else”, which a fixed script cannot offer.

8 A tap becomes a decision, in words

The card is not the tap. It is Forge's reading of the tap, written out, and putting it in words is what makes it correctable: “Run and hide” alone cannot be argued with, “the hunters come in after 30 seconds” can.

This is also what keeps the record worth keeping. After three taps the user has three sentences they can point at, rather than a wizard's memory of three clicks.

9 It says what it heard before it builds

One card, in Forge's own words, sitting between the last answer and the plan. Fix this is the whole correction path and it lives on the card, not in a menu, because if the reading is wrong here then everything after it is wrong - and this is the last cheap moment to find out.

The card also shows its hand: the props move because the user never said they should not. An inference stated out loud is one the user can refuse.

10 The plan is the same deliverable as the other two

Game type, the UEFN devices, five build steps: all three wireframes differ in where this lands, not in what it is. The first two steps are filled because they are fixed once the answers are in; the last three are the ones a user is most likely to change.

Read the card against the strip and the difference is deliberate. The plan names the three devices it leans on, and its steps also need a Player Spawn Pad and a HUD Message Device, while the strip still shows Devices and Steps dim. The strip counts what the user has said; the device list is a proposal to accept or argue with. Two doors out of the card and both are live - Build in UEFN takes the draft, Refine in chat keeps the argument going.

11 The risk, in the two places it shows

First, it reads as a quiz if the tapping never ends. Five questions of six cards is a form wearing a conversation's clothes, and the interview has to be willing to stop early and write a plan out of what it has.

Second, the free-text path is one quiet line under the cards. A user who already knows what they want has to notice it before the taps carry them along - which is why the composer is on this state too, and why the link in state 1 stays visible instead of being folded behind a “something else” card.

12 Three of five, and the two that are not

Goal, Feel and Players are filled because the user answered them. Devices and Steps stay dim because Forge proposed them and nobody has agreed yet. Keeping that distinction visible is the strip's whole job, and it is what stops 3 of 5 from being decorative progress.

13 Better on a phone, not worse

Every other structure in this set has to solve the phone. This one is native to it: six full-width cards, one thumb, the question as the first screen and no keyboard over the top of the conversation.

Two things change at 400px and neither is a loss. The brief strip drops its labels and keeps its five dots - five dots still read as “five questions to go” - and the composer stays one line instead of growing a row of buttons it does not need.

The one thing to watch: Skip — I’ll type it has to stay above the fold. It is the only way out of a question a user cannot answer, and here it is competing with six large targets for the same thumb.

What this direction bets on

Each direction in this set is a bet about which failure the chat page has to solve first. This one bets that the failure is the user rather than the box: people do not lack ideas, they lack a first move. It pays for that bet by putting words in the user's mouth, which is only safe while every one of those words can be edited.

Good at

  • Getting a first answer out of someone who has no idea what to make: one question with six words under it is a task anyone can finish.
  • Being fast on a phone, where taps beat typing and the answers are the whole screen.
  • Leaving a record. Every tap becomes a written decision in the transcript, so the brief exists in words the user can argue with later.
  • Teaching the domain by asking about it - “run and hide” is a game type's first 30 seconds, and the vocabulary is learned by using it.
  • Costing one screen to learn, against a shelf of prompts or a second panel to watch.

Risks

  • It reads as a quiz if the tapping never ends. The interview has to stop and write a plan while the user still wants to answer.
  • A quiet free-text path is a dead end for the user who already knows what they want - and the more polished the cards get, the easier that link is to miss.
  • Six taps only cover the shapes the library already knows: anything genuinely new has to survive being forced into one of six words.
  • Forge writes the user's answer for them on every card, and one wrong reading in a row costs the trust the taps were meant to earn.

What to instrument

  • Taps per session before a plan appears, and where people stop tapping: the sixth question is where the quiz feeling starts.
  • How often decision cards are edited, and which of the six answers gets corrected most - that is the reading Forge gets wrong.
  • “Surprise me” against “Skip — I’ll type it”. A screen where surprise me dominates is a screen whose questions are wrong.
  • Whether a tapped brief reaches Build in UEFN more often than a typed one, and whether the plan built from taps gets refined straight away.
